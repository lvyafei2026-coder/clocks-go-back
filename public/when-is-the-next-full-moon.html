When Is the Next Full Moon?

A full moon arrives roughly once a month, but the exact date you see on a calendar depends on where you live. The moment of fullness is a single instant worldwide, and local clocks can place it on a different day. Here is how the timing works, what each moon of the year is called, and when to look up.

How the Next Full Moon Is Dated

A full moon occurs when the Moon sits opposite the Sun from Earth, so the side facing us is fully lit. The gap from one full moon to the next averages about 29.53 days, a period known as the synodic month. Because that is shorter than most calendar months, the date generally moves about a day earlier each month.

The peak itself is a single instant, not a whole day. Almanacs publish it in UTC, and calendars convert that instant into local clock time. So the answer to when the next full moon happens can be one date in Europe and a different date in Asia or the Americas when the peak falls near midnight.

Why the Date Shifts by Time Zone

A full moon peaking at 23:40 UTC on a Tuesday is still Tuesday evening in London, but already Wednesday morning in Tokyo and Sydney. The opposite happens across the Americas: an instant just after midnight UTC lands on the previous evening in New York or Los Angeles.

This is why two reliable sources can appear to disagree. Both may be correct, because they are reporting the same instant in different zones. When you plan to look at the Moon, use the local time for your own location, and remember the Moon looks essentially full for about three nights around the peak.

Full Moon Names Month by Month

The familiar English names come largely from Native American and colonial almanac traditions. January brings the Wolf Moon, February the Snow Moon, March the Worm Moon, April the Pink Moon, May the Flower Moon, June the Strawberry Moon, July the Buck Moon, August the Sturgeon Moon, and November the Beaver Moon. December closes with the Cold Moon.

The names are traditional rather than astronomical, and the Harvest Moon is defined by timing: it is the full moon closest to the September equinox, so it sometimes lands in October. The Hunter's Moon usually follows it. Other regions and cultures use entirely different names, which is why almanacs often list several alternatives for the same moon.

Blue Moons and Years With 13 Full Moons

Most calendar months hold exactly one full moon, but because the lunar cycle runs about 29.5 days and months run 30 or 31, an occasional month catches two. The second is popularly called a blue moon. It happens roughly every two to three years, and in those years the calendar contains 13 full moons instead of the usual 12.

An older definition, still used by some almanacs, counts the third full moon in a season that contains four. Neither definition has anything to do with the Moon's colour; the term is simply a label for an extra full moon within a set period.

Best Time to See a Full Moon

A full moon rises close to sunset and sets close to sunrise, because it sits opposite the Sun in the sky. That makes moonrise the most convenient viewing window for most people: the Moon is low, easy to spot, and visible before the evening gets late.

Near the horizon the Moon can look unusually large, a well-known perceptual effect rather than a real change in size. Clear skies matter far more than hitting the exact instant, and the Moon appears nearly full on the night before and the night after the peak as well.

FAQ

When is the next full moon?

It falls about 29.5 days after the previous full moon, and the exact date depends on your time zone. A moon phase calendar set to your location gives the precise date and local time.

What time is the full moon where I live?

The full moon peaks at one instant worldwide, published in UTC. Convert that time to your local zone, and the calendar date can shift if the moment lands near midnight.

Can the full moon happen during the day?

Yes. The exact instant can occur at any hour, including daylight hours in your zone. You simply see a fully lit Moon that evening or the following one.

How often is there a blue moon?

A second full moon in one calendar month occurs roughly every two to three years. The seasonal version, a third full moon in a season with four, follows a similar rhythm.

Why does a rising full moon look so big?

It is a perceptual illusion; the Moon's actual size does not change near the horizon. Photos of a rising moon show it is no larger than one high in the sky.
